A grant to one school didn't sink the school next to it.

Across 855 rural Pakistani schools, closures rose sharply after a round of grants went out. But schools sitting next to a newly-funded competitor were no more likely to close than anyone else.

Hassan Aftab, Pitzer College · LEAPS panel data
855 schools, 266 villages, Faisalabad district
4,275 school-round observations, 2012–2014
+9–17pp rise in exit rates after the grant cycle
~0pp added exit risk for untreated schools nearby

Overall exit risk climbed. The spillover onto neighbors never showed up.

Recreated from Figure 1 in the paper. Exit stays low through round 3, then rises sharply — control villages track slightly above treated throughout, consistent with treatment modestly softening exit rather than accelerating it.

Untreated schools sit above their own survival threshold and stayed there — the grant to a neighbor didn't push them below it, whether the local market was concentrated or competitive. What did matter was baseline strength: schools with better infrastructure were more resilient, and older untreated schools were more likely to exit, consistent with displacement by newer, better-funded entrants rather than by market-wide competitive pressure.
